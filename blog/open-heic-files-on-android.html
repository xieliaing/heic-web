How to Open and Convert HEIC Files on Android

August 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Someone with an iPhone texted or emailed you a photo, and your Android phone won't open it. Instead of the picture, you get a grey box, a "can't open this file" message, or a download called something like IMG_4021.heic that no app seems to want. The photo is perfectly fine — it's just saved in Apple's HEIC format, which Android handles inconsistently.

Here's why HEIC trips up Android, and the quickest ways to actually see and convert the photo.

Why HEIC is awkward on Android

Since 2017, iPhones and iPads have saved photos as HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container) by default. The files are roughly half the size of a JPG at the same quality, which is great for the person taking the photo — but it means the picture arrives in a format built around Apple's ecosystem.

Android can read HEIC in many cases: Google added HEIF image decoding back in Android 10, and apps like Google Photos and Samsung Gallery can usually display these files today. But "usually" is the problem. Whether a given HEIC opens depends on three things at once:

  • Your Android version. Older phones (pre-Android 10) have no built-in HEIC support at all.
  • The app you're opening it in. A messaging app, a file manager, or a third-party gallery may not support HEIC even if your system technically does.
  • How the file reached you. A HEIC saved to Downloads behaves differently from one shown inline in Google Photos.

That inconsistency is why the same phone can show one HEIC fine and choke on the next.

The reliable fix: convert it to JPG

If you just need to see the photo, use it somewhere, or send it on, the surest path is to turn it into a JPG — the format every Android app, browser, and website understands. You can do this right from your phone's browser:

  1. Open the HEIC to JPG converter in Chrome (or any Android browser).
  2. Tap to add your .heic file — or drag several in at once.
  3. Download the JPG and open it in your gallery.

Because the conversion runs entirely inside your browser, the photo never leaves your phone. Nothing is uploaded to a server, there's no account, and it works the same on Android as it does on a laptop. That matters for private pictures — ID photos, documents, medical images — where uploading to some random website isn't an option.

Prefer a different format? The site also does HEIC to PNG if you need a lossless copy or transparency, and HEIC to WebP if you're putting the image on a website and want the smallest file.

Other ways to view HEIC on Android

Depending on what you have installed, you may not need to convert at all:

Try Google Photos or Files by Google

Google Photos can display HEIC images on most modern Android phones. If the file is already backed up or saved to your device, open Photos and look for it there. Files by Google and the built-in file manager on many phones can also preview HEIC.

Check your gallery app

Samsung, Google Pixel, and several other manufacturers ship galleries that read HEIF/HEIC natively. Tapping the file from the gallery — rather than from a browser download or an email attachment — often just works.

Ask the sender to change one setting

If you regularly get HEIC files from the same iPhone user, they can switch their camera to shoot JPG instead. On their iPhone: Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible. Every photo they take from then on will be a plain JPG that opens anywhere. (It won't affect photos they've already taken.)

What about opening HEIC in a browser directly?

Tempting, but unreliable. Web browsers — Chrome, Firefox, Samsung Internet — generally don't render HEIC in a normal image view, because HEIC support was largely confined to Apple's platforms. Safari on iPhones and Macs shows them; most other browsers don't. That's exactly why a converter that decodes the file for you is more dependable than hoping the browser can display it.

The bottom line

HEIC on Android is hit-or-miss: it works when your phone, your app, and the file all line up, and fails quietly when they don't. If a photo won't open, don't assume it's broken — it's almost always just the format. Converting to JPG takes a few seconds and gives you a file that opens everywhere, every time.
