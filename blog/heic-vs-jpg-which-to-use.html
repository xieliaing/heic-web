HEIC vs JPG — Which Should You Use?

October 12, 2026 · 5 min read

If you own a recent iPhone, your photos are probably saving as HEIC instead of the JPG everyone grew up with. Sooner or later you hit the question: is HEIC actually better, or should you just switch everything back to JPG? The honest answer is that each format wins at a different job — and once you see the trade-off, choosing becomes easy.

Here's a plain-English comparison, with a simple rule you can follow for the rest of the time you own the phone.

The short answer

Keep HEIC on your phone for the storage savings, and convert to JPG whenever a photo needs to leave your Apple world — going to a Windows PC, an older Android, a website upload, or anyone whose device you're not sure about. You get the smaller files day to day and a copy that opens anywhere when it matters. What each format is good at

JPEG (the .jpg or .jpeg file) has been the default photo format since the 1990s. Its one superpower is universal compatibility: every phone, camera, computer, browser, printer, and website made in the last 25 years can open a JPG without any extra software. Nothing else comes close on that front.

HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container) is what iPhones and iPads have saved by default since iOS 11 in 2017. It wraps an image compressed with the modern HEVC codec, and its headline advantage is efficiency: Apple's High Efficiency format stores a photo in roughly half the space of a comparable JPEG at similar visual quality. On a phone full of thousands of pictures, that difference is real.

But HEIC isn't only about size. The container was built for things the decades-old JPEG format can't do:

  • Richer color. HEIC can store more than 8 bits per color channel, so it holds wider color and smoother gradients — useful for HDR photos.
  • Transparency. It supports an alpha channel; JPEG does not.
  • More than one image in a file. Live Photos, burst sequences, and depth information can all live inside a single HEIC.

To understand the technology underneath, see our explainer on HEIF and HEVC.

Where HEIC falls short

The same thing that makes HEIC efficient — that modern HEVC codec — is also why it won't open everywhere. HEVC is patent-encumbered, so many platforms don't decode it out of the box:

  • Windows needs a codec from the Microsoft Store (and the key part is often a paid add-on) before it will show a HEIC. See opening HEIC on Windows 11.
  • Older Android phones may not display HEIC at all, though newer ones increasingly do — details in opening HEIC on Android.
  • Websites and apps frequently reject .heic uploads, which is a common headache we cover in why HEIC won't upload to some sites.

JPG has none of these problems. It just opens.

Quality: is one actually better?

Both formats are lossy — they discard some data to shrink the file — so neither is "perfect." In practice, at normal quality settings, you won't see a difference between a HEIC and a JPG exported from it on a phone or laptop screen. HEIC's advantage is that it reaches that quality in a smaller file, not that the picture looks visibly sharper.

A fair worry is whether converting HEIC to JPG throws away quality. The short version: at a high quality setting the loss is invisible for normal viewing, printing, and sharing. We walk through exactly why in does converting HEIC to JPG reduce quality.

A side-by-side summary

  • File size → HEIC wins (about half the space of JPEG).
  • Compatibility → JPG wins, by a mile — it opens on everything.
  • Color depth, transparency, Live Photos → HEIC wins.
  • Editing in older software → JPG wins; some apps still can't import HEIC.
  • Sharing with people on any device → JPG wins.
  • Saving storage on your phone → HEIC wins.

Notice there's no single champion. That's the whole point: HEIC is the better storage format, and JPG is the better sharing format.

So which should you use?

Follow the destination of the photo:

  • Staying on your iPhone, iPad, or Mac? Leave it as HEIC. Apple devices handle it perfectly, and you save storage.
  • Going to a Windows PC, an older phone, a website, or someone you can't check with first? Convert a copy to JPG so it opens with zero friction.
  • Going to a printer or a photo lab? JPG is the safest bet unless the service explicitly accepts HEIC.
  • Going onto a website you're building? Consider WebP for the smallest files, or JPG for guaranteed compatibility.

You don't have to pick one format forever. The smart move is to keep HEIC and make JPG copies on demand.

The trade-off is larger files and losing HEIC's extras, so many people leave the camera on High Efficiency and convert copies as needed.

The bottom line

HEIC isn't "better" or "worse" than JPG — it's optimized for a different job. HEIC saves space and carries modern features; JPG opens absolutely everywhere. Keep HEIC for storage, reach for JPG the moment a photo needs to travel outside Apple's ecosystem, and you get the best of both.
