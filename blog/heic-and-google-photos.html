HEIC and Google Photos — Viewing, Downloading, and Sharing

November 16, 2026 · 5 min read

Google Photos happily shows your iPhone pictures, so it's easy to assume everything is a normal JPG under the hood. Then you download one on your PC — or a friend saves a photo you shared — and out comes a file called IMG_4087.heic that Windows, Android, or an older photo editor refuses to open. The image looked fine in the app a second ago. What happened?

The short version: Google Photos displays Apple's HEIC format just fine, but it often hands back the original HEIC file when you download. If the thing you're sending it to can't read HEIC, that's where the trouble starts. Here's how it actually works, and the quickest way to get a JPG that opens everywhere.

The short answer

In the app, HEIC photos look normal; the friction appears when you download or share the original file. If you need a photo that opens on any device — to attach to an email, upload to a form, or hand to a Windows or Android user — convert it to JPG. Why Google Photos shows HEIC but downloads are a problem

When you view a photo inside the Google Photos app or on photos.google.com, Google decodes the image on its end and shows you a standard preview. That's why HEIC pictures look perfectly normal in Photos even on a Windows laptop or an Android phone — you're looking at Google's rendered view, not the raw file.

Downloading is different. When you choose Download, Google Photos generally gives you back the original file you uploaded. If your iPhone saved that photo as HEIC — which it does by default on every iPhone since 2017 — then the file that lands in your Downloads folder is a .heic, not a .jpg. Suddenly you're outside Google's friendly viewer and holding a format that:

  • Windows may not preview without an extra codec,
  • Android handles inconsistently depending on the app,
  • and many websites, editors, and email clients reject outright.

The photo isn't broken or corrupted. It's just in a container built around Apple's ecosystem, and you've taken it out of the one place that was quietly translating it for you.

Getting a JPG you can actually use

If you need the photo as a universally-openable file, the reliable move is to convert the downloaded HEIC to JPG:

  3. Drag the file in — or several at once — and download the JPG.

Because the conversion happens inside your browser, the picture never leaves your device. There's no upload, no account, and no copy sitting on someone else's server — which matters when the photo is a document, an ID, or anything private. Need a lossless copy or transparency instead? Sharing iPhone photos through Google Photos

Here's the good news: if you share photos within Google Photos — by sending a link or adding someone to a shared album — the recipient views them through Google's web viewer, the same rendered preview you see. They don't have to deal with HEIC at all, even on a Windows PC or an Android phone. For simply showing people your pictures, a Google Photos link sidesteps the whole format problem.

The catch returns the moment someone downloads a photo from that shared link or album. At that point they get the original HEIC file and run into the same wall you did. So if you're sharing with people who will save and reuse the images — not just look at them — it's kinder to convert to JPG first and share those.

Stop the HEIC downloads at the source

If you'd rather your iPhone stopped creating HEIC files in the first place, you can switch the camera to shoot JPG: on the iPhone, go to Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible. From then on, new photos back up to Google Photos as JPGs that download and share without any conversion step. (It won't change photos you've already taken — those stay HEIC.) There's also a middle ground: leave your camera on HEIC to save storage space, and convert individual photos to JPG only when you need to send them somewhere that can't read HEIC. For most people, that's the sweet spot — small files on your phone, compatible files when it counts.

The bottom line

Google Photos is doing you a quiet favor every time it renders a HEIC preview, which is exactly why the format catches people off guard at download time. Nothing is wrong with the photo — it's just HEIC outside its comfort zone. When you need a copy that opens on any device, in any app, on any website, convert it to JPG.
