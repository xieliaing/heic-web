HEIC and Google Photos — What Happens to Your iPhone Pictures

November 23, 2026 · 5 min read

If you back up your iPhone photos to Google Photos, you've probably never had to think about the fact that they're HEIC files. They upload, they show up on every device, and everything looks normal. Then one day you download a picture to a Windows PC or share it with someone, and suddenly you're staring at a .heic file that won't open. So what is Google Photos actually doing with your HEIC pictures — and how do you avoid the surprise?

Here's a plain-English walkthrough, with a simple fix for the one moment HEIC trips people up.

Does Google Photos accept HEIC?

Yes. Google Photos backs up HEIC photos from iPhones and iPads without any special setup. You don't need to switch your camera to JPG first, and you don't lose anything by leaving the iPhone on its default High Efficiency setting. HEIC uploads just like any other photo.

If you want to understand what the format actually is — a modern container that stores a photo in roughly half the space of a JPEG — see our explainer on what a HEIC file is.

Why your photos look fine everywhere in the app

Here's the part that fools people into thinking HEIC is a solved problem: inside the Google Photos app and at photos.google.com, your HEIC pictures display perfectly on any device — Windows, Android, a web browser, anything.

That's because Google decodes the image on its own servers and sends your screen a web-friendly version to look at. You're not viewing the raw .heic file; you're viewing Google's rendering of it. So the compatibility problem that normally comes with HEIC is hidden as long as you stay inside Google Photos.

The catch is that this convenience ends the moment the actual file leaves Google's app.

The moment HEIC bites: downloading and sharing

When you download a photo from Google Photos, you generally get back the original file — and if your iPhone saved it as HEIC, that download is a .heic. On a Mac or a modern iPhone that's no problem. On a Windows PC, an older Android phone, or inside an app that doesn't understand HEIC, that file may refuse to open.

A few things worth knowing:

  • What you get can depend on how you grab the image. Downloading the original file tends to preserve HEIC, while saving a quick preview from the browser can sometimes hand you a JPG instead. Behavior varies by device and app, so it's safest not to assume.
  • Sharing a link is fine; sharing the file may not be. A Google Photos share link shows the viewer Google's web rendering, so it always works. But if you download the file first and then email or message it, you're back to passing around a raw .heic.
  • This is the same wall you hit with website uploads. Plenty of sites reject .heic outright — a headache we cover in why HEIC won't upload to some sites.

None of this means Google Photos is doing anything wrong. It's faithfully keeping your original file. It's just that the original is a format not every device can read.

A quick reality check by device

  • iPhone, iPad, Mac → HEIC from Google Photos opens natively. Nothing to do.
  • Windows PC → the downloaded .heic often won't open without a codec; see opening HEIC on Windows 11 or just convert it to JPG for Windows.
  • Android → newer phones increasingly handle HEIC; older ones don't. Details in opening HEIC on Android.
  • Sending to someone unknown → convert to JPG first so it opens no matter what they're using.

The bottom line

Google Photos is great at hiding HEIC's one weakness — it shows your pictures beautifully on every screen because it does the decoding for you. But the file you back up and download is still HEIC, and that format only truly opens everywhere once you make a JPG copy. Keep HEIC for the storage savings, and convert when a photo needs to leave Apple's ecosystem.
